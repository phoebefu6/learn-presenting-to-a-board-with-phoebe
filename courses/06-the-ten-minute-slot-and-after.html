The ten-minute slot, and after

The paper went out five working days ago. The directors have read it, or should have. So the ten minutes are not for telling them what is on the page; they are for the ask, what has changed since, the one or two things that matter most, and then the board's questions. Tonight you plan those ten minutes, learn what the minutes and the action log will keep from them, and write the follow-up that closes every promise you made. By the end your whole slot fits on one page.

Where we start

Constructed Fernhollow Freight example. It is December. Sunniva Holm's paper on one shared customer record, €1.4 million over two years, went out in Delphine Corwen's pack five working days before the meeting. It carries three numbers, each with its rule, and the bad truth on page one. Sunniva has five likely questions with written answers and has spoken to Ingrid Ashdown, the chair. What is left is the slot itself: ten minutes on the agenda, item type "for decision". And then the part most presenters forget, which is everything that happens after the door closes: the minutes, the action log, the written follow-ups, and the first time she reports back on what the board approved.

Part 1 · covers the ten minutes: speak to the paper, do not read it

Speak to the paper, do not read it

5 min live

Directors have had the pack for days. Reading it to them spends their meeting on what they already know and leaves no time for the thing they are there to do. The working norm among company secretaries and chairs is to take the paper as read: open with the ask, say what has changed since circulation, name the one or two things that matter most, and stop. That is a practice norm, not a rule in the Code or the Act, but the FRC's guidance gives the reason behind it.

LiveThe opening sentence2 min▶

Open with the decision, in the same words as the cover sheet: "The board is asked to approve €1.4 million over two years for one shared customer record across sales, the depots and billing." Then one sentence of why now, and stop talking about the proposal itself. Directors listen differently once they know what they are being asked to do; every number that follows is heard as evidence for or against a vote.

Why it matters: the FRC's 2024 guidance to the Code (Figure 3, read at source) lists "a tendency to bring matters to the board for sign-off rather than debate" among the risk factors for poor board decisions. A presentation that fills the slot leaves the board only one move, which is to sign off. Leaving time is how you invite the debate the board owes the company.

Constructed Fernhollow Freight example: Sunniva's opening is 20 seconds. Ingrid, briefed on the pre-wiring call, then hands straight to the three numbers.

LiveWhat changed since the pack2 min▶

Five working days is long enough for something to move: a supplier quote, a month of data, a risk that got better or worse. The first minute is where you say so, in one or two sentences, and where you say plainly if nothing changed. Directors prepared on the pack; if a number they marked up has moved, they need to hear it from you before they quote the old one back.

Leaving most of the paper unspoken is not leaving it out. What you skip in the slot is detail the directors already have, never a material bad truth. If the bad news is on page one of the paper, it belongs in your three minutes too: directors must act on a fully informed basis (OECD 2023, V.A), the board's reporting must be fair, balanced and understandable (FRC Code, Principle N), and each director's duty of care (Companies Act 2006, s174) relies on what they were shown. Constructed Fernhollow Freight example: Sunniva says out loud that retention on key accounts is plausible but not measured.

Self-studySlides only if they help3 min▶

Many boards take items without slides at all; others allow one or two. Ask Delphine's equivalent, the company secretary, what yours expects. If you do use them, a slide earns its place only when it shows something the paper cannot, such as one chart the directors will want to point at during the questions. Never a slide that repeats the paper, and never a slide with a number that is not in the pack.

How to design a slide that supports the speaker rather than competing with them is taught in Public Speaking session 5 ↗, and rehearsing the slot and the questions in Public Speaking session 6 ↗. The board-specific delta is only this: the paper is the record, the slide is not.

Real world

About 21 minutes an item, on average. Reported by Comsure (2025) from a Board Intelligence survey: the average board meeting runs 3 hours 48 minutes and covers 11 items, which works out at about 21 minutes per item. That is a vendor survey, reported second-hand, so treat it as a rough picture rather than a benchmark. Even on those numbers, a ten-minute slot is a normal share of a crowded agenda, and the directors in the room have just come from another item and are heading to the next one.

Part 2 · covers the minutes, the action log and matters arising

The minutes and the action log

5 min live

Most of what is said in the room is gone by the next morning. What survives is what the company secretary writes down: the decision, why it was taken, the challenges raised, and the actions with an owner and a date. Those actions come back at the next meeting. Knowing what will be kept tells you how to answer in the room, and what you will be held to afterwards.

LiveWhat the minutes keep3 min▶

The Institute of Chartered Secretaries and Administrators' minute-taking guidance (ICSA/CGI 2017, read at source) is clear that minutes record "what was done, not what was said". They capture the key points of the discussion, the decision and the reasons for it, the key challenges raised, and a resolution line in formal wording. Actions carry an owner and a date, and are kept in a separate actions schedule that is reviewed under matters arising at the next meeting. The guidance also says actions should be board-level actions, not a list of suggestions to the presenter.

The FRC's 2024 guidance to the Code points the same way (paragraph 31, read at source): minutes should document the discussion that led to the decision, the issues raised and the reasons. That is why Tomasz's question about the CRM account lists will appear in the minutes even though most of the conversation will not. A challenge raised and answered is part of the board's record of doing its job.

What this means for you in the room: answer so that the short version can be minuted. "Rafe Okoro owns the lists; opening them is milestone one, reported before the build starts" can be written down in one line. A five-minute ramble cannot, and the company secretary will have to guess.

LiveCheck the draft minutes for your item2 min▶

The company secretary drafts the minutes and the chair approves them; the board adopts them at the next meeting. You do not own them, but you can help them be right. A short note to the company secretary on the same day, listing the actions you heard against your item with owners and dates, is welcome in most organisations. If you are sent the draft for your item, check three things: is the decision worded exactly as resolved, are the actions yours or someone else's, and are the dates the ones you said out loud.

Constructed Fernhollow Freight example: Sunniva sends Delphine two lines that evening. One action is hers (a proposed retention measure, by the January meeting); one is Rafe Okoro's (open the CRM account lists before the build starts). She does not ask Delphine to add her own talking points; minutes are not a second copy of the paper.

Self-studyDeferral is a decision too2 min▶

Not every item for decision ends in approval. A board may approve with conditions, approve part, or defer. A deferral is still a decision and should be minuted as one: what the board needs before it can decide, who will bring it, and when. If the board defers your item, ask the chair, politely and in the room, for those three things so they reach the minutes. "Come back with more" without a condition and a date is how a proposal drifts for a year.

Constructed Fernhollow Freight example: had the board deferred, the useful minute would read something like "deferred to the February meeting, pending a written plan for opening the CRM account lists, owner Rafe Okoro". That is a condition Sunniva can meet and a date she can plan to.

Real world

The actions schedule is the board's memory. The ICSA/CGI guidance (2017, read at source) recommends keeping actions in a separate schedule, reviewed under matters arising at the next meeting, rather than buried in the narrative of the minutes. In practice that schedule is the first thing many chairs read before a meeting. An action with your name on it that shows "open" for a third meeting running tells the board more about you than the slot did.

Part 3 · covers written follow-up, reporting back and the decision record

Following up

5 min live

The slot ends; the item does not. In session 5 you learned to answer "I don't know" with a date and a promise in writing. Now the promise falls due. After that comes the first time you report back on what the board approved, measured against what the paper said it would do. Both are where trust is built or spent, and both are mostly about keeping dates.

LiveThe written follow-up to "I don't know"2 min▶

Every "I don't know, I will confirm in writing by Friday" from the room is now an action with your name and a date. Send it by that date, to the company secretary for circulation to the board, not privately to the one director who asked: the question was asked of the board's paper, so the answer belongs to the whole board. Keep it short: the question as asked, the answer, the source, and what, if anything, changes in the paper's case.

If the answer is uncomfortable, it still goes in writing, and early. If you cannot make the date, say so before it passes and give a new one; the action log will show a moved date with a reason far more kindly than a silent miss. How to word what you know and do not know without overpromising is taught in Decision Intelligence, Uncertainty you can promise ↗.

LiveReporting back on what was approved3 min▶

The paper made a promise. The first report back answers one question: did it deliver what the paper said, on the numbers the board approved? Bring the same three numbers, under the same rules and the same bands you wrote in sessions 3 and 4. Do not change the rule when the result arrives. On the session 4 bench, one honest series of real NHS months printed 0 greens or 21 greens depending only on the rule chosen; a board that sees a rule changed after the fact cannot tell which of those it is looking at.

Lead with what is off track, then what is on track. Constructed Fernhollow Freight example: at the first report back Sunniva shows accounts whose lists agree against the 90% by Q3 target, wrong-address credit notes against the halving target, and spend against the year-one plan of €1.08 million, each with the rule printed beneath it. Tracking value against the business case in more depth is taught in Data Strategy session 5 ↗.

Self-studyThe decision record and the paper that was approved2 min▶

Keep three things together in one place: the version of the paper the board approved (the circulated version, not a later edit), the minute that records the decision and any conditions, and the action log entries against the item. When someone asks in a year what the board actually approved, that bundle answers, and it protects you if the project's scope drifts. If the paper changed between circulation and the meeting, the minutes should say which version was approved; check that with the company secretary.

The memo side of following up, telling the people who were not in the room what was decided and what happens next, is taught in Executive Briefings, Send, land, follow up ↗.

Real world

A follow-up that saves a director time is respected. Spencer Stuart's 2024 Director Pulse Survey (read at source, 751 US directors) found directors spending about 200 hours a year on board work, and 242 for public-company directors. That is a few weeks of working time across every paper, meeting and committee. A short, dated, written answer that closes an action without a meeting is the kind of help a director with that load notices. The sample is US directors; UK and other boards may differ.

Plan the follow-up and what goes on the action log. Each likely action with an owner and a date, and the date of the first report back.

★ Step 7 · after the meetingSame day: two lines to Delphine with the actions heard. Now your own slot: the same eight lines. Fill each one for your item. If a line is empty, that is the work to do before your pack deadline.

★ Step 8 · your slot1 The ask: The board is asked to ... 2 Type and money: for decision / discussion / assurance / noting 3 Three numbers: each with its green, amber and red rule 4 The bad truth: not claimed ... / main risk ... 5 Five questions: asker, kind, one-line answer 6 Ten-minute plan: 0-1, 1-4, 4-10 7 Chair note: needs, expecting, timing 8 Follow-up: actions, owners, dates, first report back

Read it aloud once, then put it away. Time yourself saying the first two blocks of the plan. If it runs past four minutes, cut words, not the bad truth. On the night, the page stays on the table for the questions; you do not read from it.

Homework

Before your board meeting

30 min
  1. Finish your one-page slot: the eight lines from step 8, filled for a real item, fitting on one side.
  2. Time your first four minutes out loud, twice. Cut until you leave at least half the slot for questions.
  3. Ask your company secretary three things: whether slides are expected, how actions are recorded, and whether you will see the draft minutes for your item.
  4. Put the follow-up dates in your calendar now: the same-day note, the written answers within a week, the next meeting's matters arising, and the first report back.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Sunniva has a ten-minute slot for an item for decision, and the pack went out five working days ago. What is the best use of the ten minutes?

Directors have read the pack, so the slot is for the decision, what has moved, what matters most, and their questions. Reading aloud leaves no time for debate, which the FRC guidance names as a risk factor. New material on the night takes away their preparation time, and the bad truth is never skipped: they must act fully informed.

2 · What should the minutes record about Sunniva's item?

The ICSA/CGI guidance asks minutes to record what was done, not what was said: key points, the decision and its reasons, key challenges, and actions with owner and date. The FRC guidance (paragraph 31) adds the discussion that led to the decision. Actions are board-level, not a list of suggestions to the presenter.

3 · In the room, Sunniva promised Halvard: "I will confirm in writing by Friday." What should she do next?

A promise made in the room becomes an action with an owner and a date. Sending it on time, to the board through the company secretary, lets the action log show it closed at matters arising. An informal word reaches one director; a late or silent answer teaches the board that her dates do not hold.
