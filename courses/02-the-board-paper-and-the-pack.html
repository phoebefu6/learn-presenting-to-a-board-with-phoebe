The board paper and the pack

Most of the work of a board slot happens days before the meeting, on paper. Directors read your item in a pack alongside everything else on the agenda, often in one long evening, and they arrive with their minds half made up. Tonight we cover the four rules the governance guidance sets for a board paper, the one-page cover sheet that sits on top of it, and the timetable that decides whether your paper is read at all. By the end you have a filled cover sheet for your own item.

Part 1 · covers the FRC rules for board papers, and showing the challenge

Four rules for a board paper

5 min live

A management paper persuades people who will carry out the work. A board paper informs people who will answer for the decision without doing the work, and who cannot ring you at ten at night to ask what a column means. The FRC's guidance to the Code turns that into four plain rules, and each layer of a well-built paper serves one or two of them.

LiveThe four rules in FRC paragraph 783 min▶

The FRC's Corporate Governance Code Guidance (2024, read at source) sets out, in paragraph 78, what good board papers do. In paraphrase, they are: accurate, clear, comprehensive and up to date; they carry a summary of the contents; they say what is expected of the director, whether a decision, a discussion or information only; and they are delivered sufficiently in advance of the meeting for the director to read and think.

Notice what the list does not say. It does not set a page count, it does not ask for a slide deck, and it does not reward persuasion. Three of the four rules are about the reader's ability to act: understanding what is in front of them, finding the point quickly, and having the time. Constructed Fernhollow Freight example: Sunniva's draft runs to nine pages with the cost table in the middle and no line saying what the board is asked to do. It fails the third rule before Tomasz reaches page two.

LiveShow the challenge already done2 min▶

Paragraph 31 of the same FRC guidance asks that papers describe how a proposal was developed and how it was challenged before it reached the board. A director with a care duty under section 174 is not only asking "is this a good idea?" but "has anyone already tried to break it?" Saying who tested the numbers, which options were rejected and why, and what the executive pushed back on is not padding. It is evidence that the board is seeing a tested proposal rather than a first draft.

Chris Hodge's guide for ICSA, A Guide to Better Board Reporting (2018, read at source via Mondaq), frames board reporting around a set of "Twelve questions" a paper should help the board answer. Use it as a checklist after you draft, not as a template to fill: if a director would ask one of the questions and your paper is silent, add a line. Constructed Fernhollow Freight example: Sunniva adds one sentence that Halvard Lind, the CFO, checked the cost table, and one listing the two options she considered and rejected.

Self-studyWhat a poor paper costs the board2 min▶

Figure 3 of the FRC guidance lists risk factors for poor board decisions, and a weak paper is named directly among them. The reason is mechanical, not stylistic. Directors decide on what the pack gives them. A paper that buries the ask, mixes current and stale figures, or leaves out how it was tested makes the decision worse even when the proposal is sound.

Before you send a draft to the company secretary, read it as Ingrid would: open the cover sheet, read the summary, close the paper, and say out loud what you are being asked to decide and why. If you cannot, neither can she.

Real world

The regulator names it. Among its risk factors for poor board decisions, the FRC's 2024 guidance to the Code lists "Poor quality papers" (Figure 3, read at source). The paper is not a formality around the decision; in the regulator's own list, it is one of the things that can make the decision go wrong.

Part 2 · covers the cover sheet: GGI's eight elements and the CGI warning

One page on top: the cover sheet

5 min live

Many boards ask for a standard cover sheet on every paper, usually owned by the company secretary. It is the page that tells a director, in under a minute, why the item is there, what kind of attention it needs and what they are being asked to do. Fill it last, after the paper is written, and it becomes the best test of whether the paper knows its own point.

LiveGGI's eight elements3 min▶

The Good Governance Institute's note Brilliant basics: the report cover sheet (May 2026, read at source) sets out a one-page cover sheet with eight elements: purpose; board role (decision, discussion, assurance or noting); an executive summary; the recommendation; strategic alignment; key risks and implications; equality, sustainability and public interest; and assurance and next steps. GGI is an advisory body, not a regulator, so treat the list as well-tested practice rather than a rule. Your company secretary's own template wins.

Two details do most of the work. The purpose test is one question, and it is GGI's: "why is this in front of the board, now?" And the risks slot asks for two or three material risks, not a register. A cover sheet that lists twelve risks has handed the director the job of deciding which matter.

LiveWhere the options, and the rejected ones, go2 min▶

GGI does not list options as one of its eight elements. That does not mean options are optional. A board deciding on a single proposal with no alternatives in view is being asked to sign off, not to decide, and session 1 showed that the FRC names sign-off rather than debate as a risk factor. So put the options in the executive summary, one line each, and give them fuller treatment in the body with the reason each was rejected.

Constructed Fernhollow Freight example: Sunniva's summary names two rejected options. A one-off clean of the three customer lists was cheaper but drifts back within a year. Making the CRM the master record was considered and rejected. One line each on the cover sheet, a short paragraph each in the body.

Self-studyThe cover sheet is not the paper2 min▶

The Chartered Governance Institute's specimen cover sheet for charity board meetings (2023, public introduction read at source; the template itself is members-only) makes a point every author should hear: the cover sheet helps directors navigate, but they are still expected to read all the information in the paper, not only the cover. A cover sheet that says something the body does not support is worse than no cover sheet.

The practical consequence: never put a claim on the cover sheet that is not backed in the body, and never let the cover sheet soften what the body says. If the body says the main risk is that the CRM account lists launch empty, the cover sheet says the same, in the same words.

Real world

One question decides the first slot. GGI's purpose test asks why the item is in front of the board now. Constructed Fernhollow Freight example: Sunniva's answer has two halves. Why the board: the spend is above the CEO's delegated limit. Why now: build has to start in January for the first depot to go live in Q3, and the next board meeting is too late.

Part 3 · covers circulation timing, pack length and what moves to an appendix

The pack, and when it lands

5 min live

Your paper does not travel alone. It goes out in a pack with every other item on the agenda, on a date the company secretary sets, and it is read in whatever time the director has left. The date decides your real deadline, and the size of the pack decides how hard you have to work to be read at all.

LiveFive working days, and why2 min▶

The CGI's charity cover-sheet note (2023, public introduction read at source) says papers are circulated "usually five working days before the meeting", and warns against packs so large that directors cannot get through them. Five days is a common norm, not a law; your company secretary sets the timetable, and the FRC guidance places the management of information flows to the board with the company secretary (paragraph 82). Ask for the date the day you know you have a slot.

Then count backwards. The circulation date is not your deadline; it is the company secretary's. Before it come the sign-offs: the CFO checks every number, the sponsoring executive confirms the paper says what the executive approved, the company secretary checks the template and the item type. Constructed Fernhollow Freight example: Sunniva finishes drafting fifteen working days out so that Halvard and Delphine each have time to send it back once.

A paper that misses the pack is either tabled on the day, which breaks the fourth FRC rule because no director has read it, or deferred to the next meeting. For Sunniva, deferral means build does not start in January.

LiveHow long packs are: reported, and disputed3 min▶

Every figure on pack length and reading time is reported, mostly from surveys run by Board Intelligence, a board-reporting software vendor. Read them that way.

  • ICSA and Board Intelligence (2017), reported by Chris Hodge on Mondaq: large organisations produce about 2,000 pages of board papers a year; nearly three-quarters of respondents said packs were too long, and 68% too operational.
  • Board Intelligence and CGI (2026), reported on the Board Intelligence blog: packs average 220 pages, up 27%.
  • Board Intelligence, reported by Comsure (2025): directors spend 3 to 4 hours on a pack, at about 30 pages an hour.

Now the disagreement. NACD's 2024 board practices survey (read at source) found 62% of directors call their board materials very or extremely effective. The 2026 Board Intelligence and CGI work (reported) found 62% rate packs weak or poor. Same number, opposite verdicts. NACD asked public-company directors one question; Board Intelligence, with CGI, asked its own sample a different one, and Board Intelligence sells board-reporting software. Different samples, different questions. Neither settles how good packs are in general, and neither tells you how long yours should be. What both support is narrower: directors read a lot, under time pressure, so the first page has to work.

Self-studyWhat goes to an appendix, or the reading room3 min▶

An appendix holds the working a director may want to check: the cost build-up, the data behind a chart, the method. A reading room (a separate folder in the board portal, or a paper file at the meeting) holds material for reference only, such as a full vendor proposal or a project plan. Neither is a place to hide anything. Ask the company secretary what your board uses.

The test for moving something out of the body is whether it helps a director oversee the decision. Operational detail usually does not, and moving it out helps them. A material problem always does, and it stays in the body and on the cover sheet. The reasons are the ones from session 1: the OECD Principles expect directors to act on a fully informed basis (V.A), the Code asks the board for a fair, balanced and understandable assessment (Principle N), and a director's care duty under section 174 assumes the paper can be relied on. Constructed Fernhollow Freight example: the field-by-field mapping goes to an appendix; the risk that the CRM lists launch empty stays on page one.

Real world

Two surveys, one number, opposite stories. NACD (2024, read at source): 62% of directors rate board materials very or extremely effective. Board Intelligence and CGI (2026, reported by Board Intelligence): 62% rate packs weak or poor. When you quote either to a board, say who ran it and who they asked.

Build-along

Sunniva's cover sheet, and yours beside it

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Take your board map from session 1 and fill the eight slots for your own item, one line each, while we fill Sunniva's. Write the paper first if you have one; if not, the cover sheet will tell you what the paper has to contain. Constructed Fernhollow Freight example throughout: the people and figures are invented.

Purpose: why here, and why now. Two halves. Why the board (the reserved matter or the limit) and why this meeting (what slips if it waits).

★ Step 1 · purposePurpose: The spend is above the CEO's delegated limit, so it is reserved to the board. Build must start in January for the first depot to go live in Q3; the next meeting is too late. (Constructed Fernhollow Freight example.)

Board role. Carry the type over from your board map. If it has changed since session 1, find out why before you write another word.

★ Step 2 · board roleBoard role: For decision Not: for noting (the executive's approval does not settle a matter reserved to the board)

Executive summary in three sentences. The decision, the cost, the main risk. If it needs a fourth, the fourth is the rejected options.

★ Step 3 · executive summary1. One shared customer record for sales, the depots and billing, approved by the executive on 14 November. 2. Cost: €1.4 million over two years (platform and build €900,000 then €200,000; depot and sales time €180,000 then €120,000). 3. Main risk: the CRM account lists are not opened up early, so the platform launches empty.

The recommendation sentence. "The board is asked to ..." with one verb and one object. It is the sentence that will appear in the minutes if the board agrees.

★ Step 4 · recommendationThe board is asked to approve spend of €1.4 million over two years for one shared customer record across sales, the depots and billing, with build starting in January.

Two material risks, linked to the recommendation. Each risk says what would go wrong if the board approves, and who owns it. Two or three, never a register.

★ Step 5 · key risksRisk 1: CRM account lists not opened up early; the platform launches empty. Owner: Rafe Okoro, commercial. Risk 2: depot staff time during the cut-over. Owner: Bram Tully, depot planning, with Odile Mercer, COO.

Options considered and rejected, one line each. These go in the executive summary and the body, not in a slot of their own. A board that sees the alternatives can decide rather than sign off.

★ Step 6 · rejected optionsRejected: a one-off clean of the three lists (cheaper; drifts back within a year). Rejected: making the CRM the master record (considered and rejected; reasons in the body).

Assurance and next steps. Who checked the numbers, which committee sees what, and what happens after approval. This is where you show the challenge already done.

★ Step 7 · assurance and next stepsChecked: cost table checked by Halvard Lind, CFO. Committee: data protection to Tomasz Wexley's audit and risk committee before build starts. Next: build starts January; first depot live in Q3.

Decide what goes to the appendix. Detail that does not help oversight moves out; anything material stays in the body and on this page.

★ Step 8 · appendix, not bodyAppendix: cost build-up by quarter; field-by-field mapping; the 2,140-account match sample. Stays in: the empty-launch risk, the depot time cost, and that retention on key accounts is plausible but NOT measured.

Read it as Ingrid. Cover the body, read only your eight lines, and ask: could a director vote on this, and would they know what they had voted for? If not, fix the page, not the speech. Homework

Before session 3

30 min
  1. Finish your cover sheet for one real item: all eight slots, one line each, with the recommendation sentence starting "The board is asked to ...".
  2. Get your board's own template and timetable from the company secretary: the cover-sheet format, the circulation date for your meeting, and who signs off before it.
  3. Count back from the circulation date and write the date you must finish drafting, allowing one round of comments for each sign-off.
  4. Sort your paper into body and appendix. For each thing you move out, write one line on why it does not help oversight. If you cannot, it stays in.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Which of these is NOT one of the rules for board papers in paragraph 78 of the FRC's 2024 Code Guidance?

Paragraph 78 asks for accurate, clear, comprehensive and up-to-date papers, a summary, a statement of what is expected of the director, and delivery sufficiently in advance. It sets no page limit. The one-page cover sheet is practice from bodies such as GGI, sitting on top of a longer paper.

2 · GGI's cover sheet has eight elements and options are not one of them. Where should Sunniva put the options she rejected?

Options belong in the summary and the body: a board shown only one proposal is being asked to sign off rather than decide, and FRC Guidance paragraph 31 asks papers to show how proposals were developed and challenged. The recommendation slot still carries the one decision requested.

3 · NACD (2024) found 62% of directors rate board materials very or extremely effective; Board Intelligence (2026, reported) found 62% rate packs weak or poor. What can you fairly take from this?

The two surveys asked different people different questions, and one comes from a vendor. Teach the disagreement rather than pick a winner. The reported pack lengths (about 2,000 pages a year; 220 pages on average) and reading times (3 to 4 hours) say packs are heavy, which is why the first page has to work.
